MotionCanvas

A hand-tracking drawing tool that treats tremor as a source of expressive texture, instead of an error to correct.

My role
Technical lead: prototyping, interaction design, calibration
Team
3 students

01 · The problem

Most drawing tools assume a steady hand.

Mainstream creative tools such as Adobe Fresco and Procreate assume steady, precise hand control and treat involuntary movement as an error. For people with motor differences, every stroke becomes a fight with the interface.

We asked a different question: what if the tool adapted to the person, instead of the person adapting to the tool? MotionCanvas treats movement as the creative material.

02 · Who it's for

Adults with motor differences who want to make art.

The target users are adults with motor differences, including essential tremor, multiple sclerosis, cerebral palsy and limited range of motion. Three design goals came from that:

  • Expressiveness. Natural movement, including tremor, should produce engaging, intentional artwork.
  • Adaptability. The system should fit each person's movement range, not use fixed defaults.
  • Emotional engagement. People should feel they are using a creative tool, not an accessibility device.

We could not get direct access to users. Our research was a literature review, a comparison of existing tools and a speculative storyboard. The full research, sources and evaluation are in the group document.

03 · Research and framing

Designing around ability, not limitation.

Ability-based design

Our main framework was Wobbrock et al. (2011): instead of asking what a user can't do, start from what they can do and make the system adapt. Hendren (2020) sharpened this for us by arguing that the tools, not the users, are the problem.

Embodied interaction

Dourish (2001) and Kendon (2004) treat movement as meaningful and expressive. Drawing with the whole hand in the air makes the body part of the artwork.

The key reframe: tremor isn't noise to filter out. It's a signal we can turn into texture.

04 · Design process

Three iterations, each evaluated before the next.

1

April 2026 · HTML5 canvas, checked by a group review

BuiltMouse drawing, five colour swatches, a size slider, save and clear. The mouse stood in for the Leap sensor. A tremor button was in the interface but did nothing.

LearnedThe drawing basics worked, but the tremor button was a false affordance: it looked real and changed nothing, so the central idea could not be tested. There was no onboarding or calibration, and the look was generic.

ChangedMove to p5.js for easier brushes and animation. Add onboarding and calibration. Build real tremor detection. Give the app its own visual identity.

2

May 2026 · full p5.js rebuild, checked by a cognitive walkthrough

BuiltOnboarding, calibration with a live range display, four brushes (smooth, particle, scatter, ink), working tremor detection drawing cross-hatch, and a Simulate Tremor mode so the idea could be shown without a sensor.

LearnedNo undo. No success state after calibration. A side panel that was hard to scan. The tremor idea was not explained in context, sensor status never updated, and light mode only covered the canvas.

ChangedAdd multi-step undo and redo, label the tremor reframe, show live sensor status, group the panel, and theme the whole interface.

3

May 2026 · submission prototype, internal evaluation

BuiltUndo and redo (up to 20 steps), an eraser, a Clear button, a bigger palette, light mode as the default, a panel titled "Movement - Tremor Reframe" with a numeric intensity readout, a live sensor status pill, and hover-and-hold selection through the Leap sensor.

LearnedUndo and redo fixed the biggest frustration. Naming the tremor reframe in the interface changed how clearly the tool communicates its purpose. But the Clear button has no confirmation, calibration success is only a small corner label, and hover-and-hold behaved differently from laptop to laptop.

NextTest with users who have motor differences, add audio feedback, and test gesture detection properly with real hand movement.

05 · The final design

Calibrate, then draw. Every movement counts.

Adaptive calibration

For 10 seconds the user moves their hand through their natural range. The app records the smallest and largest positions and maps that box to the canvas, so a small range of movement can still reach the whole page.

Tremor becomes texture

When tremor mode is on, fast movement that stays within a tiny area is read as tremor and drawn as cross-hatch marks. Stronger tremor gives more, longer, wider marks, so the movement becomes part of the user's style.

Mid-air drawing

Leap Motion hand tracking, connected through the TouchFree SDK, lets users draw without holding or touching anything. Hover-and-hold selection lets users pick tools without touching anything, and a mouse fallback keeps the app usable without the sensor.

06 · Outcome and reflection

What worked, and what I'd do next.

Tutor feedback

The strongest feedback was on process. Our three iteration cycles were rated excellent, with clear documentation, rich video and screenshot evidence, and a good evaluation method. The reflection on group tools and the personal reflection were both praised, including the point that working without real users is a real limit in accessibility research.

The feedback also named what was weaker, and I agree with it:

  • User research was thin. Our usability goals were too open-ended, with no clear way to measure them. Next time I would write goals that can be checked, such as how long a new user takes to reach their first stroke, how many complete calibration, and how often they use undo.
  • The literature was broad but not specific enough. We covered disability and the social model well. We needed more on 2D drawing, embodied drawing and related interaction techniques.
  • Evaluation needed to go beyond technical checks. Each round should be tied to a stated criterion, and show how it changed the design.

Limits I want to be upfront about

  • Iterations 2 and 3 used a Simulate Tremor toggle. It does not reflect real essential tremor, MS or cerebral palsy movement, so the detection has not been validated against real users.
  • Hover-and-hold with the Leap sensor worked on some laptops and not others, and gesture detection was not fully tested with real hand movement. Mouse mode was fully tested.

What I learned as technical lead

  • How to turn a design principle, ability-based design, into concrete technical decisions such as calibration.
  • That hardware differs between machines. A design that works on one laptop can fail on another, so a fallback input mattered.
  • How much accuracy matters. When I checked our process diagrams against the code, I found real mistakes: the sensor connects through the TouchFree SDK, calibration is a bounding box and not a matrix, and the tremor logic only runs when tremor mode is on.

If I had more time

  • Run co-design sessions with adults who have motor differences, before adding more features. Using real users at early prototype stage has trade-offs: recruiting and consent take time, an unfinished prototype can frustrate participants, and small samples limit what the findings prove. I would plan for those from the start, with short sessions and clear consent.
  • Add audio feedback, so the tool works as more than a visual interface.
  • Add a confirmation step to the Clear button, and a clearer calibration success message.
